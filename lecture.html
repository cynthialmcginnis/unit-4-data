Opening problem

A fraud model that never catches fraud

A bank has 5,000,000 card transactions. Investigators confirmed that 40 of them were fraud.

The bank trains a model to flag fraud. The model learns one rule: "Every transaction is legitimate." It never flags anything.

Accuracy is the share of all predictions a model gets right. If it labels 100 transactions and 95 labels are correct, its accuracy is 95 percent.
What you do: Decide what accuracy this model earns. Pick one answer. The explanation appears below your choice.

4,999,960 correct ÷ 5,000,000 total = 99.9992 percent

Accuracy

0 of 40
The one thing the bank built it to do.
  • Plenty of data is not the same as enough data. The bank had 5 million records and only 40 examples of the thing it needed the model to learn.
  • One score can hide a weak spot. The better question: "How many of the 40 fraud cases did it catch?"

Section 1 · Formats

Structured, unstructured, or semi-structured?

Structured

Rows and columns with a fixed meaning for each column. Most traditional machine learning runs on it.

Unstructured

No fixed fields. Emails, photos, voice recordings. Industry estimates put 80 to 90 percent of organizational data here.

Semi-structured

Some labeled fields wrapped around free content. An email has sender, date, and subject fields around a free-text body.

Section 1 · Labels and amounts

Labels cost money. Rare examples cost more.

Most business AI learns from labeled examples: this email is spam, this X-ray shows pneumonia, this transaction was fraud. A person usually creates each label.

Labeling 10,000 medical images can take radiologists months. When an AI project stalls, missing or unreliable labels are a common cause.

Cold start problem: a brand-new user or product has no history, so a recommendation system has nothing to go on until data builds up.

The minimum test: does the data include enough examples of every situation the system must handle?

Section 2

Five dimensions of data quality

"Good data" is too vague to act on. Quality breaks into five things you can check.

Perfect data does not exist. The goal is to know where your data is weak.

Section 2 · Data quality

Five questions to ask any dataset

DimensionThe question it asksHow you check it
AccuracyDo the values match reality?Compare a sample against a trusted source. Flag impossible values, such as an age of 212.
CompletenessIs anything missing?Count missing values in each column. Then ask which records are missing, not just how many.
ConsistencyIs the same thing recorded the same way everywhere?Look for mixed formats: "USMC" (short for United States Marine Corps) and "Marine Corps" in one column, pounds and kilograms mixed.
TimelinessIs the data current enough for the purpose?Check the date range. Shopping data from before 2020 describes a world that no longer exists.
RepresentativenessDoes the data match the people the system will serve?Compare each group's share of the data with its share of the real population.
Software can count blanks and flag strange values. It cannot tell you whether a value makes sense. A resting heart rate of 38 looks like an error until a cardiologist tells you it is normal for a marathon runner. Quality checks need a tool and a person who knows the subject.

    Section 3

    Where bias hides in data

    Data bias means the data misrepresents the world the same way every time, so the model learns it as a rule.

    In Unit 2 you looked at the ethics of bias. Tonight is about mechanics: where in the data the bias comes from.

    Section 3 · Five types

    Five types of data bias

    TypeWhere it comes fromExample
    SelectionThe way data was collected leaves some people or situations out.Trained on city hospitals, used in rural clinics
    RepresentationSome groups are in the data, but too rarely for the model to learn them well.Speech recognition with more errors for speakers it rarely heard
    MeasurementA variable is measured badly, or measures the wrong thing.Health spending used to stand for health need
    HistoricalThe data accurately records an unfair past.Lending decisions made under discriminatory rules
    ProxyA harmless-looking variable stands in for a sensitive one.ZIP code standing in for race
    A proxy is a variable that stands in for something else. An employment gap can stand in for caregiving, illness, a spouse's deployment, or a permanent change of station (PCS) move.

    Section 3 · Feedback loop

    When biased data feeds itself

    Two neighborhoods have the same amount of real crime: 200 crimes a year each. Neighborhood A starts with more police patrols. Arrests happen where police are, so A produces more arrests. A model reads the arrests and assigns next year's patrols, giving a little extra to whichever area looks riskier.

    What you do: Predict what share of the patrols A will have after five years. Then select Run another year five times. Real crime never changes.
    Share of patrols in A
    A 60%
    Share of patrols in B
    B 40%
    Year 0
    The output variable was wrongThe model treated arrests as a measure of crime. Arrests measure where police are. That is measurement bias.
    The model fed itselfEach year the model's decisions produced the data it learned from next year. 60 versus 40 grew to 82 versus 18.
    How to break the loopUse a measure that does not depend on patrols, such as crimes reported by victims. Check decisions by neighborhood every year.

    How to find bias before training

    1. Count by group. Compare each group's share of the data with its share of the real population.
    2. Check the labels by group. If one group is labeled "high risk" far more often, ask whether the label reflects reality or how the data was collected.
    3. Ask what each variable really measures. What else could it stand in for?
    4. Ask the people affected. They spot proxies analysts miss.

    Section 4 · Outliers

    Outliers: error or real?

    An outlier is a value far from the rest. Every outlier is one of two things:

    • An error: a value that cannot be true, such as a typing mistake or a broken sensor. Fix it or remove it.
    • Real but unusual: a true value that is rare. Keep it. A fraud model exists to find unusual transactions, so deleting every outlier deletes the fraud.
    What you do: For each value, decide: is it an error to fix, or a real value to keep?

    Score: 0 / 6

    Rule: before you remove an outlier, find out what caused it.

    Section 4 · Scaling · 3D

    Scaling: why 500 flight hours swamp 3 faults

    Some models compare records by measuring the distance between them. k-nearest neighbors is the classic example: it labels a new record by looking at the most similar records it already knows. If one variable uses big numbers, it controls the distance.

    Normalization squeezes every value into the range 0 to 1: (value − minimum) ÷ (maximum − minimum). Standardization centers each variable at 0: (value − average) ÷ standard deviation. Use standardization when the data has outliers.

    Section 6

    Testing a dataset before you trust it

    In 2016 Microsoft released a chatbot called Tay on Twitter. It learned from the people it talked to. Within a day, users taught it to post racist and hateful messages, and Microsoft shut it down.

    No one had set up a way to screen what Tay learned from. Testing a dataset is that screen: the checkpoint between collecting data and training a model.

    Section 6 · Profiling

    A summary of every column

    A profile lists a few numbers for each column before anyone reads individual rows. Here is the profile of a personnel dataset with 2,400 records.

    What you do: Find four problems in this profile. Then select Show the four problems to check.
    ColumnAverageSmallest to largestSpread (standard deviation)MissingDistinct values
    age2718 to 586041
    months_of_service740 to 9,00021012312
    branch(text)(text)(text)05
    • Largest months of service is 9,000. That is 750 years. An error, perhaps 90 typed as 9,000.
    • The spread is 210 months, almost three times the average. A few extreme values are pulling it up.
    • 12 records are missing months of service. Next question: which 12, and do they have something in common?
    • Branch has 5 distinct values at an installation with 4 branches. "USMC" and "Marine Corps" are counted as two.

    The profile found four problems without anyone reading 2,400 rows. Profiling, a picture of the data, a count by group, and rules from someone who knows the subject catch most problems.

    Section 6 · Cross-validation · 3D

    Cross-validation: five scores instead of one

    One train-test split gives one score, and that score may be lucky. In k-fold cross-validation you divide the data into k equal parts, called folds. Here k is 5. In each round, the model trains on four folds and is tested on the fifth. After five rounds, every fold has been the test fold once, so you get five scores.

    The example: a model that predicts whether a clinic patient will show up or miss an appointment. The data is 5,000 past appointments. Each fold holds 1,000. The score is the percent of test-fold appointments the model predicted correctly.

    Section 6 · A vendor's score is not your score

    The sepsis alert

    Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection.

    The tool. Epic, a company whose electronic health record system is used in many U.S. hospitals, built a model that sends an alert when it predicts a patient is developing sepsis. Epic reported strong accuracy on records from other hospitals.

    The check. University of Michigan researchers tested the same model on their own hospital's patients and published the results in 2021.

    Sepsis cases the alert missed
    About 2 of 3
    Hospitalized patients who got an alert
    About 1 in 5
    Most of them did not have sepsis. When most alerts are false, staff learn to ignore them.

    The model did not change. The set of patients did: a different hospital, different patients, different record keeping. That is the representativeness problem from Section 2.

    The lesson: a score only tells you how a model did on the data it was tested on. Ask whose data produced it, then test the model on your own.

    Wrap-up

    What to take from tonight

    Choose

    • Match the kind of data to the problem first.
    • Check all five quality dimensions separately.
    • Missing data is rarely random.
    • Bias hides in how data was collected, measured, and labeled.

    Prepare

    • Every cleaning step is a choice. Write it down.
    • Scale variables that use very different units. Use one-hot encoding when categories have no order.
    • Find the cause of an outlier before you remove it.
    • A new input variable can beat every column you started with.

    Split and test

    • Split before you prepare. Split by person or by time when it matters.
    • One score can hide a weak spot.
    • Test on your own data.
